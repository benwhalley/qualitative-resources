Build an argument

Why build an argument?

Good research writing is an argument, not a list of points. Every section except the Method makes claims, backs them with evidence, and says how to interpret that evidence. Each step has to follow from the one before, and the reader has to be able to see how.

In the Introduction, the claims build towards your research question: what we know, what kind of evidence that is, what it can't tell us (the gap), and so what your study asks. The connectives between claims carry the logic: for example, however, as a result.

In the Results, each theme is a claim. The evidence is your extracts, and the interpretation after each one says what it shows. Without it, extracts just pile up and the reader has to do the analysis for you.

These activities let you practise this on real papers:

  • Body Hair: put the claims from a published Introduction in order, and link them.
  • Green Space: build an Introduction for a made-up study from real sources, and write the gap yourself.
  • Qualitative themes: rebuild a published theme: claim, then extract and interpretation, extract and interpretation.
